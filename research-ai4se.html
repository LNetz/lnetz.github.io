How does a language model produce text, why is it sometimes confidently wrong — and how do we make its output reliable enough for software to build on? From tokens to agents to language models that write models.

Series1Model-driven engineering2AI in software engineering3Digital twins4Evolutionary algorithms

Ask a chatbot for the data model of a small library app — books, members, loans — as JSON. What comes back looks right at first glance. Hand it to a program, and sometimes it fails: a closing brace is missing, a key has lost its quotes, or a friendly sentence comes before the JSON.

AI4SE also includes requirements work, testing, review and debugging. This article focuses on LLM-assisted modeling and model-driven generation, and answers three questions about that case. How does a language model produce such text? Why is it sometimes wrong — and just as confident when it is? And how do we make its output reliably right, so that a program, such as the generators of part 1, can build on it?

01What an LLM does

An LLM — a large language model — has learned one thing extremely well: to continue a text. Answering, translating and writing code all follow from that. This chapter goes from the outside in: first what you see in the chat window, then what happens inside.

One token at a time

To answer, an LLM does one thing over and over: given the text so far, it computes how likely each possible next piece of text is. Such a piece is called a token — a word, part of a word or a punctuation mark. One token is drawn, appended, and generation continues with that context (usually reusing cached attention keys and values) [1]:

Why not always take the most likely token? Because then the text tends to become flat and repetitive; a little randomness makes it livelier. The price: the same prompt can give different answers, and nothing in the output says why a token was chosen. The temperature sets how much randomness there is — turn it up, and the variety grows, first in the wording, then in the facts.

For the curiousTokens and sampling
  • Where tokens come from. The vocabulary is not designed by hand. Byte-pair encoding starts from character or byte units, depending on the tokenizer and keeps merging the most frequent neighbouring pair into a new token [2]. Frequent words end up as one token, rare ones as several pieces. That is also why LLMs are clumsy at counting letters: variable token boundaries make character counting less direct.
  • Temperature. The model actually outputs a score per token; the probabilities come from them by a softmax, after dividing every score by the temperature. Below 1 the likely tokens get even likelier; above 1 the distribution flattens and rare tokens get a chance. At temperature 0 the most likely token is always taken.
  • Top-k and top-p. Most systems also cut off the long tail before drawing: only the k best tokens, or only the smallest set whose probabilities add up to p (“nucleus sampling”) [3]. That keeps the variety of sampling without the occasional absurd token. The demo above keeps only the candidates it shows.

Words become numbers: embeddings

Inside, the model works with numbers, not words. Every token becomes a vector — a long list of numbers — called an embedding. The following two-dimensional toy illustration is inspired by classic static word embeddings, not a measured GPT token space:

Transformer token representations become context-dependent across layers. Semantic search commonly uses a separate embedding model for whole queries and passages: a sentence about a happy dog and one about a playful hound land close together, although they share no word. That makes semantic search possible — finding a passage by what it means, not by its exact words. Chapter 02 builds on it.

For the curiousDimensions and distance
  • How many dimensions? The figure has two; real embeddings have hundreds to thousands — GPT-3 works with 12,288 numbers per token [4]. Nobody chose what each number means; the axes are whatever the training found useful.
  • Close means similar. Closeness is usually measured as the angle between two vectors (cosine similarity), not their distance — a long and a short text on the same topic point the same way.
  • Arithmetic with meaning. In word embeddings of 2013, the vector of king minus man plus woman landed closest to queen [5].

Inside: a neural network

What turns those numbers into the next token is a neural network. The idea is older than it looks — the first one, the perceptron, dates from 1958 [6] — and easiest to see with a picture: is this a dog or a cat?

The network does not see a dog, only pixel values, one number per input. Every connection has a weight: a number saying how much one input counts. In this toy network, hidden neurons use ReLU: negative weighted sums become zero. Softmax converts the two output scores into probabilities.

Training adjusts the weights over many examples until the right output wins. For scale: the roundworm C. elegans gets by with 302 neurons; GPT-3 has 175 billion weights (parameters and biological neurons are different quantities) [4].

For the curiousHow the weights are learned
  • How the weights move. After each example the network compares its output with the right answer. Backpropagation works out, layer by layer from the back, how much each weight contributed to the error [7]; every weight is then nudged a tiny step in the direction that makes the error smaller (gradient descent). Repeat that billions of times.
  • Pre-training. An LLM needs no labelled pictures: the right answer is simply the next token of a real text. This enables learning from large collections of unlabelled text.
  • Becoming an assistant. A pre-trained model only continues text. Possible post-training stages include instruction tuning and preference optimization such as RLHF: people rank answers, a reward model learns those preferences, and the LLM is tuned towards them (RLHF). In the InstructGPT study, people preferred the answers of a tuned model with 1.3 billion weights over those of GPT-3 with 175 billion [8].

What made it work: attention and scale

Which earlier words matter for the next one? In 2017 a new architecture, the transformer, made that question its core [9]: every token gets to look at every earlier token and weigh how much it matters. This is called attention. For the curiousInside the transformer
  • What attention computes. Every token is turned into three vectors: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I offer?) and a value (what do I pass on?). A token compares its query with the keys of all earlier tokens; the better they match, the more of their value it takes in. In the toy illustration, this is the backward-looking relation shown for “big”, not a measured explanation.
  • Many heads, many layers. Several such attention “heads” run side by side, each free to learn a different kind of relation, and the layers are stacked — 96 of them in GPT-3, each 12,288 numbers wide.
  • The context window. Standard full attention over an input sequence has quadratic cost. Cached decoding and sparse or sliding attention have different costs. Context limits also reflect training, architecture and serving choices — 2,048 for GPT-3, hundreds of thousands for current models. Whatever does not fit, the model does not see.

Plausible, not true: hallucinations

An LLM picks the most plausible continuation, not the true one. Models can generalize beyond training examples, and can also fail on familiar facts. Fluent wording and training-data coverage do not establish factual correctness.

For developers the best-documented case is code that imports packages that do not exist. A study generated 576,000 code samples in Python and JavaScript with 16 models: on average, at least 5.2 % of the packages that commercial models referenced did not exist, and 21.7 % for open-source models — 205,474 different invented names [11]. Anyone can register such a name in a package registry and put malicious code behind it.

Reasoning is just as fragile. “Alice has 5 sisters and 3 brothers. How many sisters does a brother of Alice have?” — the best models of 2024 often got this wrong [12]. Tools close part of the gap: a code interpreter, a lookup in the package index, a test run. An LLM that uses tools in a loop is an agent.

For the curiousWhy models guess

Why does a model not simply say “I don’t know”? One answer: training and evaluation reward guessing. In a test that scores right answers and gives nothing for “I don’t know”, a guess is always the better bet — for students in an exam and for language models in a benchmark. Kalai et al. argue that hallucinations follow from exactly this, and that fewer of them need different scoring: wrong answers must cost more than an honest “I don’t know” [13].

THREE THINGS TO KEEP
  • An LLM writes one token at a time, each one drawn from a probability distribution.
  • Plausible is not true: expect confident mistakes, especially beyond the training data.
  • Because it writes token by token, every single token can be checked — chapter 03 uses exactly that.

02Building around the model

A model alone turns text into text. Most of what makes AI useful in practice is built around it: search, tools, loops and checks. First, the vocabulary.

The vocabulary

I propose to consider the question, “Can machines think?”

Alan M. Turing · Computing Machinery and Intelligence, 1950

The question is older than computers as we know them [14], and “artificial intelligence” has stayed a broad and loosely defined term: even simple decision trees are often called AI. Machine learning narrows it to algorithms that learn from data, deep learning to those that use neural networks, generative AI to systems that create new content. Generative AI describes a capability, including non-deep-learning methods. LLMs are a major class of deep-learning-based generative models; ChatGPT and Copilot are products combining models and software:

And four words that come up whenever LLMs are used:

Prompt
Everything the model gets as input: the instructions (system prompt), the conversation so far, attached documents.
Context window
How many tokens the model can take in at once. Whatever does not fit, it does not see.
Few-shot
Examples of questions and answers in the prompt that show the model what is wanted — without training it again.
Fine-tuning
Training an existing model further on one’s own data. Needs good data and hardware, and the result stays fixed until the next training.

RAG: give it your documents

An LLM can use both learned patterns and information supplied in its context or through tools. To let it answer from your own documents, you do not train it again — you search first and hand it what you found. Combined with the semantic search of chapter 01, this is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [15]:

Users can describe their problem in their own words and still find the right passage. SE-Chatty, a chatbot on lecture material, works this way.

For the curiousChunks, index, re-ranking
  • Before the first question. The documents are cut into chunks of a few paragraphs, each chunk is turned into an embedding, and the embeddings go into a vector index.
  • At every question. The question is embedded the same way; the index returns the nearest chunks; they are pasted into the prompt together with the question and the instruction to answer from them and to name the source.
  • Where it goes wrong. Both retrieval and generation can fail. The model can ignore relevant evidence or invent citations; retrieval can fail through chunks cut in the wrong place, a question that uses other words than the documents, a table that loses its structure. Many systems therefore combine semantic search with plain keyword search and let a second model re-rank the hits.

Agents: tools in a loop

Agent

An AI agent is a language model that uses tools in a loop to reach a goal — and decides for itself which step comes next.

Chatbots may also use tools. Here, an agent means a goal-directed tool-using LLM with autonomy over its next steps. An agent gets a goal, acts — reads a file, runs the tests, edits code —, looks at the result and decides what to do next, until the goal is reached. Simon Willison puts it in one line: “An LLM agent runs tools in a loop to achieve a goal.” [16]

Anthropic draws the line against workflows: in a workflow, LLMs and tools follow paths a programmer laid down in advance; in an agent, the LLM directs its own process and its use of tools [17]. That makes agents flexible — and harder to predict.

Harness: everything except the model

Harness

A harness is all the software around a language model that turns it into a working agent — everything in the agent except the model itself.

The word comes from the harness of a horse. The horse brings the strength, but only the harness — collar, reins, the cart behind — turns that strength into directed, useful work. A language model on its own can only turn text into text. The harness runs the loop of gathering context, acting, checking the result and going again, and around it provides instructions, tools, context, guardrails and checks. Hence the short formula: agent = model + harness.

Retrieved documents, repository files and tool results are task data, not trusted instructions. The harness needs tool permissions, isolation and approval gates for consequential actions; syntax checks alone do not prevent prompt injection or unsafe execution.

Part of the harness comes built in; teams add their own on top — project rules, extra tools, their own checks. Birgitta Böckeler sorts these additions into guides, which steer the agent before it acts, and sensors, which observe afterwards and help it correct itself [19].

For anyone building with AI, this is the practical lesson: much of what makes an agent good or bad sits in the harness, not in the model. ESACA, the project I work on, is such a harness for companies — coding agents inside the company’s own perimeter. And the next chapter shows two harness parts for models: a parser as a sensor, a grammar as a guide.

03Making output reliable: LLMs and models

Copilot-style assistants write lines of code. This chapter goes one level up: the LLM writes the model, and a generator writes the code. What models, languages and generators are is explained in part 1; here the question is how to make the LLM’s part reliable. The example is the one from part 1: a small library with books, members and loans.

Language models that write models

A modelling language is a language like any other, so you can teach it to an LLM without retraining: put a few example models into the prompt (few-shot) and let it look up the language reference when it needs it (RAG) [BDMN+24]. The examples here use CD4A, a compact text notation for UML class diagrams (see part 1). LLMs tuned for a modelling language can do more than create new models: they can change existing ones, or turn a whiteboard sketch into proper syntax. When they edit, though, they sometimes change things nobody asked for — and sometimes repair errors along the way [NKR+25]. Either way, something has to check the result.

Check afterwards: the parser

What makes this reliable is what happens after the LLM. A parser — the same one the generator uses — checks every model. If it fails, the model goes back to the LLM together with the parser’s error message, and the LLM tries again:

This is the harness idea from chapter 02, applied to models: the parser is a sensor. It catches every syntax error — but only afterwards, and every new attempt costs time.

Prevent upfront: constrained decoding

Syntax errors can also be prevented during generation. An LLM writes one token at a time, so before each token is chosen, a grammar can remove every candidate that cannot follow at this point. This is called constrained decoding. It is easiest to see with JSON: after an opening brace, only a quoted key or a closing brace may follow.

Candidates in this demo are schematic readable fragments, not actual tokenizer units. JSON needs quotes, colons, commas and valid values, not just balanced braces. The CD4A tab shows the same mechanism with the grammar of a modelling language, building the Book class of the library. For nested braces the grammar keeps a count of what is still open — exactly where LLMs are weak: even ChatGPT has trouble closing brackets correctly in long texts [1].

Case study: grammar masking for CD4A. We tried this with small open models that run on a single computer, on about 1,000 modelling tasks [NRR24]:

With masking, parser acceptance rises to 87–93 %, from 0.3–42 %. Our 2024 MontiCore → Lark → Guidance prototype did not reach 100 %: converted grammar constructs differed from the target parser; token limits and nonterminating repetitions also caused failures. Runs reaching the token limit are excluded from the table. In this Guidance-based setup, generation took 13–30 times as long; this is not a general cost of constrained decoding. Semantic well-formedness and requirements still need separate checks.

The mask needs control over the decoding loop. With open models you have it yourself. Hosted APIs now do part of it on their side: several can guarantee output that follows a JSON schema, and OpenAI’s API also accepts a context-free grammar for custom tools [20].

For the curiousWhy the mask is a guarantee
  • Why it is a guarantee. A parser afterwards can only reject; the mask decides beforehand. With a sound constraint engine, an exact target grammar and normal termination at an accepting state, the output belongs to the enforced grammar. Truncation and grammar-conversion errors break these preconditions; syntax alone does not establish semantics [21].
  • Tokens do not match the grammar. A token can contain half a keyword, or a brace plus a space plus the next name. So the mask cannot simply look up grammar symbols; it checks for every candidate whether its characters can continue the text so far. Doing that fast for tens of thousands of tokens at every step is the engineering part.
  • Syntax, not meaning. Under those preconditions the mask ensures syntactic acceptance. Whether the classes are the right ones, or whether a referenced class exists at all, still needs checks afterwards — and the model can be pushed into valid but awkward text when it “wanted” something else.

From model to application

Put together with model-driven engineering, this goes all the way: users describe their domain informally, an LLM derives the class diagram, syntax and semantic checks validate the model, and a generator such as MontiGem turns it into a runnable data-centric web prototype [Net24]:

The LLM only does what it is good at: turning language into a model. Reproducible generation can still reproduce template defects. Business rules, workflows, authorization and integrations may require additional models and extensions. Accurate grammar conversion, timeouts, contextual constraints and generator maintenance remain engineering tasks. The explicit intermediate model lets people check what the AI understood — before a single line of code exists.

In short

  • An LLM continues text, one token at a time, each drawn from a probability distribution.
  • It writes the plausible text, not the true one — expect confident mistakes, such as packages that do not exist.
  • RAG, agents and harnesses are built around the model; much of an agent’s quality sits in the harness.
  • Checking belongs in the harness: tests, linters, parsers.
  • A sound decoder can guarantee syntax for the enforced grammar if it finishes normally; semantics and requirements remain separate checks.
WORTH IT WHEN
  • the output is in a formal language — JSON, a DSL, code — that a parser or a grammar can check
  • a program builds on the output
SOMETHING ELSE IS ENOUGH WHEN
  • the text is for people only: then a careful human review does the job
