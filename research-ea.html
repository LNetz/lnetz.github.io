02Evolutionary Algorithms

article · 2026

Evolution as an algorithm: many guesses, a score, and a few rules for which guesses get to have children — what evolutionary algorithms are, how each step works, and when to use them.

Series · part 1: Model-driven SEpart 2: AI-driven SEpart 3: Digital twinspart 4: Evolutionary algorithms

Where evolutionary algorithms sitHow an evolutionary algorithm worksEvolutionary algorithms in practice

Place 12 wind turbines on a square kilometre, each on one of 100 cells of 100 × 100 m. There are 1.05 · 1015 ways to choose the cells, before any spacing rule — checking a million layouts per second would take 33 years. Yet any single layout can be scored in an instant: so much energy, so much loss in the wake of the neighbours. When you can rate every candidate but cannot list them all, you can let the candidates evolve.

An evolutionary algorithm keeps a population of candidates. It rates each by its fitness, lets the better ones have children — mixing their parts (crossover) and changing them a little at random (mutation) — and lets the worse ones die out (selection). Generation by generation, the population drifts toward good solutions. In code, it is a loop of a few lines:

population = [random_candidate() for _ in range(20)]
for generation in range(60):
    scored  = [(fitness(c), c) for c in population]
    parents = select(scored)                       # the better ones, more often
    kids    = [mutate(crossover(a, b)) for a, b in pairs(parents)]
    population = survivors(scored, kids) + [random_candidate(), random_candidate()]
best = max(population, key=fitness)

This article explains that loop, step by step, on one running example: a toy wind farm, the problem of my master’s thesis [Net17] in small. First chapter: where evolutionary algorithms sit next to the machine learning and LLMs of part 2. Second: what each step does — with figures that run real, replayable evolutions. Third: the family, two applications from my own work, and how to try it yourself.

01Where evolutionary algorithms sit

Evolutionary algorithms are older than deep learning and work quite differently. To place them, ask what a method needs — and what it gives back.

The AI map

“AI” covers methods that have little in common. Some get a formal model of the problem — rules, constraints — and search it: route planners, SAT and SMT solvers. Some get examples and learn a model from them: regression, deep networks, LLMs. And some get only a score: a way to rate any candidate, without knowing why one is better. Evolutionary algorithms belong to the third group. The standard AI textbook files them under search and optimisation, next to hill climbing and simulated annealing [4], not under learning.

The border to machine learning is open, though. Evolution can tune the settings of a model or even grow its network — and if you have used Optuna for hyperparameters, you may already have run an evolutionary algorithm: its CMA-ES and NSGA-II samplers are exactly that.

Genetic, local, global: three words that mislead

“Genetic” is a metaphor. Holland’s genetic algorithms [2] borrowed the words — chromosome, gene, crossover, mutation — not the biology. Eiben and Smith warn that reading evolution as optimisation is natural but “somewhat misleading” [1]: nature has no fixed goal. This article says evolutionary algorithm for the whole family and genetic algorithm for the variant with chromosomes and crossover — the one of my thesis.

Local or global? The textbook calls these methods “local search” because they keep only current candidates, not the paths to them [4]. A population still explores many regions at once — my thesis puts it as evolving “towards multiple optima in parallel” [Net17].

Learning from data vs searching with a score

A neural network learns by gradient descent: it needs examples with answers and a loss that says, for every weight, in which direction the error shrinks. An evolutionary algorithm needs neither. It needs a score for a candidate — which may itself come from data or from a slow simulation — and it gives back good candidates: a layout, a schedule, a test suite. Nothing is learned that would generalise to the next problem.

Both use randomness on purpose. In part 2, the temperature decides how often a language model picks a less likely word. In an evolutionary algorithm, randomness enters twice: in selection, which decides how strongly the better candidates are preferred — the selection pressure, which even has a temperature form (chapter 02) — and in mutation, which invents what no parent had.

The two worlds meet in a new way. In FunSearch [16] and AlphaEvolve [17], building on the idea of evolution through large models [15], the candidates are programs: an LLM reads a few good ones and writes a variant, an automatic evaluator scores it, the best are kept and varied again. The LLM replaces the hand-written mutation — the loop and the score stay exactly as described here.

No free lunch

Is there a best optimiser? No: averaged over all possible problems, every black-box optimiser performs the same — Wolpert and Macready’s No Free Lunch theorems [5]. An evolutionary algorithm that knows nothing about your problem has no advantage over random search. The advantage comes from what you build in: an encoding where good parts can be combined, operators that respect the constraints, a score that rewards the right thing. Chapter 02 is about exactly these choices.

And one family, the evolution strategies, is closer to gradient descent than it seems: “ES can be seen as computing a finite difference derivative estimate in a randomly chosen direction” [7].

Evolution in software engineering

In 2001, Harman and Jones proposed to treat software-engineering problems the same way: find a representation for a candidate and a fitness to score it, and let a search do the rest — search-based software engineering [8]. Two examples show how far this goes.

  • Test generation. EvoSuite evolves whole test suites for Java classes. A suite is scored by what it misses: uncovered methods plus the branch distance — for if (x >= 10) run with x = 5, the distance to the missing branch is 10 − 5 + k, a small k > 0 ensuring that a branch that was only just missed still counts as uncovered — so the search gets a slope toward the branch instead of a flat “not covered” [9].
  • Program repair. GenProg evolves patches until the failing test passes; it fixed 55 of 105 bugs for about eight dollars each [10]. A later analysis found that most of these patches were not correct — many simply deleted the faulty functionality, and a system that only deletes produced at least as many correct patches [11]. The tests were the fitness, and the tests did not forbid deleting.

That second story is the most important lesson of this article in one sentence: evolution optimises exactly what you measure. The same search also speeds up existing programs — genetic improvement made a 50,000-line bioinformatics tool 70 times faster on average on its benchmark [12] — and tests apps at scale: Sapienz found 558 unknown crashes in the top 1,000 Android apps [13].

02How an evolutionary algorithm works

The running example: a site of 1 × 1 km, 12 turbines, at least 240 m apart (three rotor diameters). The toy score, taken from the test objectives of my thesis, says that the wind is better toward the north-east: a turbine in the south-west corner scores 0, one in the north-east corner 1; a layout’s fitness is the mean over its turbines. Wakes are off for now — they come back in the fitness section. A run starts with 20 random layouts and evolves them for 60 generations.

The loop

One pass through the loop is a generation; the candidates of a generation are the population; one candidate is a chromosome, its parts are genes. Each generation, the algorithm checks whether to stop, selects parents, pairs them up, recombines each pair into a child, mutates some children, scores them, drops the worst of parents and children, and adds a few random newcomers. Step through one real generation:

Every library draws this loop a little differently, but the shape is always the same, and it can be folded into three verbs: ask for new candidates (select, pair, recombine, mutate), evaluate them, tell the algorithm the scores (it decides who survives). That is how libraries such as CMA-ES present it. In geneial, the library of my thesis, each step is an exchangeable building block with its own builder. Two words will come up in every section:

Selection pressure

How strongly the better candidates are preferred. Too little, and the search drifts; too much, and the population collapses onto the first good idea.

Exploration vs exploitation

Searching new regions vs refining near what is known to be good. Every knob of the loop moves the balance between the two [1].

Overlapping generations, plus and comma

Not every algorithm replaces its whole population each generation. Here, as in geneial, 6 children compete with 20 parents and only the 8 worst of all 26 go: generations overlap, and because the best always survive, the algorithm is elitist by construction. Evolution strategies name the two choices: (μ + λ) selects the survivors from parents and children together, (μ, λ) only from the children — the parents are forgotten, which helps to escape from a good-looking spot [18].

Encoding: what a candidate is

The genotype is what the algorithm manipulates; the phenotype is what it means. For the wind farm, the obvious genotype is a list of 12 positions. They can be free floating-point coordinates — or coordinates snapped to the centres of a grid of ρ × ρ cells. My thesis compared exactly these two grid genes and float genes [Net17]. The grid is a design decision with consequences you may not expect:

The lesson of the thesis, in its own words: “There is no benefit in using a resolution that exceeds the one of the objective function” [Net17] — resolve no finer than your score can tell apart. And the encoding decides which operators make sense. A list of numbers in a fixed order — a schedule, second by second — can be cut and spliced. A set of positions has no order: which turbine is “gene 3”? It needs its own crossover (below). The first population comes from a chromosome factory: it draws random positions one by one and reverts any that break the spacing, so that every candidate is valid from the start.

How big is the search space, honestly?

The textbook encoding for wind farms is one bit per cell: 1 = a turbine stands here [28]. It is intuitive and fine for coarse grids. But the thesis did the arithmetic for a fine one: at ρ = 10,000, a single chromosome has 108 bits, and a population of 1000 would need hundreds of gigabytes [Net17]. A list of coordinates stays the same size however fine the grid — 12 pairs of numbers. And a fun fact on precision: two neighbouring 64-bit floating-point numbers near 1000 m are about 10−13 m apart — far below the size of an atom. No wind model resolves that.

Fitness: the score is the specification

The fitness function is the only thing the algorithm knows about your problem. In the real wind farm it is a chain of models: wind statistics, the wake behind each turbine, the power curve, and finally money — energy per year, cost per megawatt hour, net present value or rate of return [Net17]. Such a simulation can take “several minutes up to days” per candidate [Net17], which is why the thesis also has toy scores like ours. Here are the wakes switched on:

Three practical points. Budget: the cost of a run is the number of evaluations, roughly population × generations — our toy run needs 20 + 60 × 8 = 500 (six children and two newcomers per generation), at one minute each more than eight hours; evaluate in parallel and cache scores. Constraints: invalid candidates can be repaired (the factory, the crossover below), penalised in the score, or made impossible by the encoding (the hybrid car in chapter 03). Direction: this article maximises; many libraries minimise — negate the score.

And the most important point, again: the score is the specification. The GenProg patches passed the tests by deleting features; the hybrid-car controller in chapter 03 used more fuel when told to care only about fuel.

When the specification is right: the NASA antenna

A good specification can make evolution beat engineers. For NASA’s Space Technology 5 mission, Lohn and colleagues evolved the shape of a small wire antenna. To survive manufacturing tolerances, every design was simulated several times with small random perturbations of its angles and wire radii — and its fitness was the worst of these runs [25]. The result looked like a bent paperclip, met the requirements, and flew on the mission in 2006 — the first evolved hardware in space [26].

Selection: who gets to have children

Selection turns fitness into chances. The classic roulette wheel gives each candidate a slice proportional to its fitness [3], as in my thesis [Net17]. It has a weakness you can try below: it depends on the absolute numbers. A tournament draws k candidates at random and takes the best — it only compares, and k is a single dial for the pressure [6]. A softmax with a temperature sits in between, exactly like the token choice of a language model.

Elitism copies the best candidates unchanged into the next generation, so the best score can never get worse. But selection should not only pick the best: the thesis recommends to “select a few ‘bad’ chromosomes” as well, because the diversity “is needed to break free from local maxima” [Net17].

Takeover time

How fast does selection alone — without crossover and mutation — fill a population with copies of the best? Goldberg and Deb called this the takeover time and compared the common schemes: proportional selection is slow when the scores are close together, tournament and ranking selection take over in a number of generations that grows only with the logarithm of the population size [6]. Fast takeover is good for exploitation and bad for exploration — the same balance again.

Pairing and crossover

The selected parents are paired at random — in my thesis this is the coupling step; one parent can be in several pairs. Crossover then builds a child from both: the hope is that good parts of two parents combine into something better than either. How depends entirely on the encoding.

One-Step crossover does not create a single new position; it only recombines. Problem-aware operators like this one beat generic ones in my thesis [Net17] — but only because the simulation reports a score for every turbine. If your evaluator returns one number per candidate, there is nothing to rank by. The general rule: if your evaluator can give partial scores, use them.

Building blocks, carefully

Why should crossover work at all? Holland’s schema theorem [2] gives a lower bound for how often short, above-average patterns of genes — schemata — are expected to appear in the next generation. That much is a theorem, under its assumptions. The popular follow-up, the building-block hypothesis — that GAs work by assembling such good blocks into ever better solutions — is contested [1]. One thing is certain and easy to check: crossover does not change how often a value occurs at a position across the population; it only reshuffles the combinations. New values come only from mutation and newcomers.

Mutation: three knobs

Mutation changes a child at random, a little. It is the only step that invents something no parent had — and it can also spoil a good child, which is why my thesis does not mutate every child but chooses some first [Net17]. “Mutation rate” sounds like one setting; in fact there are three:

What the thesis found: mutation lowers the average fitness of a population — and without it, runs freeze at different levels, depending on luck. For float genes, big steps helped early and small ones late, but “the extend of the influence of the mutation rate is small” [Net17]. Mutation is also the slot that the LLMs from chapter 01 fill: in FunSearch and AlphaEvolve, the “mutation” is a language model rewriting a program.

Step sizes that tune themselves

Choosing the step size by hand is tedious, so evolution strategies let it adapt. Rechenberg’s 1/5 success rule: if more than a fifth of the mutations improve, take bigger steps; if fewer, smaller ones [18]. Self-adaptation puts the step size into the genome, so that it evolves along. CMA-ES goes furthest: it learns a whole covariance matrix — which directions are promising and how they correlate — from the path the search has taken [19].

Survivors, newcomers and diversity

After the children are scored, the worst of parents and children are dropped, and a few random newcomers from the factory are added — after the culling, “to maintain a sufficient variety” [Net17]: they are almost always below average and would not survive a fair comparison. Why bother? Because “an evolutionary algorithm converges when the diversity of its population breaks down and most of its individuals become similar” [Net17] — and if that happens too early, it has converged on the wrong hill. Three ways to read such charts — they are worth logging in any real run. Diversity flat early: too much pressure. The mean no better than random: too many newcomers, the run has become random search. And always compare against random search with the same number of evaluations: here it found the right hill in all 10 runs — but reached its top in only 2 of them, the balanced setting in 6. A good evolutionary algorithm explores and refines.

Keeping diversity on purpose

Random newcomers are only one way to keep diversity. Fitness sharing divides the score of candidates that crowd the same spot, so that a crowded hill becomes less attractive; crowding lets a child replace the parent it resembles most, so that niches survive [1]. Novelty search goes all the way and rewards being different instead of being good — and still finds good solutions on deceptive problems [24]. Its descendants, such as MAP-Elites, keep an archive of the best candidate for each kind of behaviour (chapter 03).

When to stop

In most real problems you do not know the best possible score — the optimum is “simply unknown” [Net17]. So runs stop after a budget: a number of generations, of evaluations, or of seconds; or when the best has not improved for a while; or when a target is reached. Criteria can be combined. The hybrid car in chapter 03 stops after exactly 100 generations, because the controller must answer in fixed time [NWN+15]. Evolutionary algorithms are anytime algorithms: you can stop them whenever you like and take the best so far — and most of the gain usually comes early, as the charts above show.

SENSIBLE FIRST SETTINGS

The setup my thesis recommends for grid genes [Net17] — a starting point, not a law: 100 candidates; half of them selected as parents; an elite of 10; 10 % of the children mutated, moving 1 to 5 genes; 10 random newcomers per generation. Then: run at least ten seeds, log best, mean and diversity, and beat random search with the same budget.

Tuning the tuner

Who tunes the tuner? Every setting above is itself a parameter of the search, and choosing them is an optimisation problem of its own — my thesis notes that GA configurations are often tuned by GAs [Net17]. The field distinguishes parameter tuning, fixing the settings before a run, from parameter control, changing them during the run, as the self-adapting step sizes do [1]. One idea from the thesis’s future work: start with a coarse grid and refine it while the run converges.

03Evolutionary algorithms in practice

The family beyond the genetic algorithm, two problems from my own work, and how to start.

The family

“Evolutionary algorithm” is a family name. Its members differ mainly in two choices: what a candidate is — which decides how it can be varied — and what you want back — which decides what survives.

  • Genetic algorithms (GA): strings of genes, crossover as the main operator [2] [3] — the dialect of this article.
  • Evolution strategies (ES) and CMA-ES: real-valued vectors, mutation with adapting step sizes [18] [19]. The default for continuous parameters.
  • Differential evolution (DE): mutates by adding the difference of two other candidates, then crosses with the parent and keeps the better of the two [20].
  • Genetic programming (GP): candidates are programs — the basis of GenProg and genetic improvement.
  • NSGA-II: several objectives at once; returns a Pareto front of trade-offs instead of one best [21]. In model-driven engineering, PerOpteryx uses it to optimise software architecture models for performance, reliability and cost [14] — see part 1 for models.
  • MAP-Elites and novelty search: keep the best candidate for every kind of behaviour [22] — AlphaEvolve’s program database is inspired by it [17].
  • NEAT and neuroevolution: evolve neural networks, topology included [23].
Evolution at scale

Evolution strategies also scale like few other methods. Because each candidate is scored independently and only scores need to be exchanged, Salimans and colleagues ran ES on 1,440 workers and taught a simulated humanoid to walk “in under 10 minutes” — a competitive alternative to reinforcement learning with gradients [7]. David Ha’s visual guide shows several of these strategies side by side on 2-D landscapes [29].

Case: energy management in a hybrid car

A plug-in hybrid has two engines on one axle: a combustion engine and an electric motor. Every 0.02 seconds its controller decides how to split the requested torque between them — burn fuel now, or use the battery and recharge it later? In the DFG project OASys at RWTH’s Theory of Hybrid Systems group, we built a controller around a genetic algorithm [NWN+15]; geneial started as its framework.

The weights are the specification, as in chapter 01: telling the GA to care only about fuel gave a worse fuel result than adding “keep the battery near its target” — a plausible reading is that the battery term adds foresight beyond the 20-second window. With the best weighting, the GA used up to 1.8 % less fuel than the best of the published reference controllers it was compared with [NWN+15]. Why a GA inside a controller?

Why a GA inside a control loop, where every step has a deadline? Because it is an anytime algorithm with a fixed budget: 50 candidates for 100 generations fit into the time step. Because its population carries over from step to step — shifted by one gene, it is already a good guess for the next decision. And because the fitness can be any mix of goals, including ones that are hard to put into a formula for a classical controller. A larger population starts and ends higher, but costs time; 50 was the compromise for real time [NWN+15].

Case: offshore wind-farm layout

The real version of our toy: Horns Rev 1 in the Danish North Sea, 80 Vestas V80 turbines on 3.8 × 5 km, wind data of eight years, rated by annual energy production, net present value and internal rate of return [Net17]. Genetic algorithms are a classic tool for placing wind turbines [28]; the question of my thesis was narrower: should the positions be free floating-point numbers or points on a grid? The thesis set the grid to a 0.1 m spacing — fine, but not finer than the model can use — and compared both encodings, each with the configuration recommended for it:

The thesis concludes that discretised genes are “much more efficient and yield better results in all test cases” [Net17]. Try it yourself

The whole algorithm of the convergence figure, in plain Python without any library — the “balanced” setting on the two hills. Copy it, run it, then change one knob at a time:

import math, random

def fitness(g):  # two hills: broad and low, narrow and high
    x, y = g
    low = 0.6 * math.exp(-((x - 0.25)**2 + (y - 0.30)**2) / 0.08)
    high = 1.0 * math.exp(-((x - 0.80)**2 + (y - 0.75)**2) / 0.006)
    return max(low, high)

def new():  # initialisation: a random candidate
    return [random.random(), random.random()]

def run(seed, size=20, gens=60, elite=2, newcomers=2, k=3, p_mut=0.3, step=0.05):
    random.seed(seed)
    evals = 0
    def score(cands):  # evaluate each candidate once
        nonlocal evals
        evals += len(cands)
        return [(fitness(g), g) for g in cands]
    pop = sorted(score([new() for _ in range(size)]), reverse=True)
    for gen in range(gens):  # stop: a fixed budget of generations
        def pick():  # tournament selection
            return max(random.sample(pop, k))[1]
        kids = []
        for _ in range(size - elite - newcomers):
            a, b = pick(), pick()
            # uniform crossover: each gene from one of the two parents
            child = [random.choice(p) for p in zip(a, b)]
            if random.random() < p_mut:  # mutate some children
                i = random.randrange(2)
                child[i] = min(1, max(0, child[i] + random.gauss(0, step)))
            kids.append(child)
        kids += [new() for _ in range(newcomers)]  # random newcomers
        # elitism + replacement: the best survive, the rest is replaced
        pop = sorted(pop[:elite] + score(kids), reverse=True)
    return pop[0], evals

for seed in range(1, 11):
    (f, best), evals = run(seed)
    print(seed, [round(v, 2) for v in best], round(f, 3), evals)
WHAT YOU WILL SEE

Seeds 4, 5 and 7 end on the low hill with fitness 0.600; seeds 1, 2, 3, 6, 8, 9 and 10 reach the high one (≥ 0.99). Every run costs 1,100 evaluations. Three out of ten on the wrong hill — that is why you run seeds. Try newcomers=0, k=8 or p_mut=0 and count again.

For real problems, use a library. They differ in what they make easy:

The ask–tell loop of chapter 02, literally, with CMA-ES for two real numbers (adapted from the cmaes README):

import numpy as np
from cmaes import CMA

def quadratic(x1, x2):
    return (x1 - 3) ** 2 + (10 * (x2 + 2)) ** 2

optimizer = CMA(mean=np.zeros(2), sigma=1.3)
for generation in range(50):
    solutions = []
    for _ in range(optimizer.population_size):
        x = optimizer.ask()                     # propose
        solutions.append((x, quadratic(x[0], x[1])))   # score
    optimizer.tell(solutions)                   # keep the better, adapt

To watch evolution without writing code, try Genetic Cars 2 ↗, where a GA breeds two-wheeled cars and lets you change the mutation rate and the elite while they drive. The library behind the figures of my thesis, geneial, is open source (C++11, MIT) [30] but no longer maintained. And if your system already has a digital twin, an optimiser like these can become one of its services.

In short

  • One loop with exchangeable parts: propose candidates, score them, keep the better ones, repeat. You write the encoding and the score; libraries supply the rest.
  • No gradient, no training data — only a score. The price is evaluations: population × generations, each possibly a slow simulation.
  • The encoding is the main design decision. It decides which operators make sense and how big the space is; resolve no finer than the score can tell apart.
  • The score is the specification. Evolution optimises exactly what you measure — patches that delete features, a fuel-only controller that uses more fuel.
  • Selection pressure works against diversity. Every knob moves the balance between exploring and exploiting; a run that loses its diversity has converged, right or wrong.
  • Randomness is a tool, so measure it: run several seeds, log best, mean and diversity, and compare with random search.
WORTH IT WHEN
  • you can score a candidate, but there is no gradient
  • the space is huge, discrete or mixed, with many local optima
  • several objectives pull against each other
  • hundreds to thousands of evaluations are affordable, and “very good” is good enough
SOMETHING ELSE IS ENOUGH WHEN
  • gradients exist, or the problem is convex
  • an exact solver fits the problem — SAT, SMT, integer programming (see SMT-RAT)
  • only a few dozen evaluations are affordable
  • you need a guarantee of optimality
