Evolution as an algorithm: many guesses, a score, and a few rules for which guesses get to have children — what evolutionary algorithms are, how each step works, and when to use them.

Place 12 wind turbines on a square kilometre, each on one of 100 cells of 100 × 100 m. There are 1.05 · 1015 ways to choose the cells, before any spacing rule — checking a million layouts per second would take 33 years. Yet any single layout can be scored in an instant: so much energy, so much loss in the wake of the neighbours. When you can rate every candidate but cannot list them all, you can let the candidates evolve.

An evolutionary algorithm keeps a population of candidates. It rates each by its fitness, lets the better ones have children — mixing their parts (crossover) and changing them a little at random (mutation) — and lets the worse ones die out (selection). Generation by generation, the population drifts toward good solutions. In code, it is a loop of a few lines:

population = [random_candidate() for _ in range(20)]
for generation in range(60):
    scored  = [(fitness(c), c) for c in population]
    parents = select(scored)                       # the better ones, more often
    kids    = [mutate(crossover(a, b)) for a, b in pairs(parents)]
    population = survivors(scored, kids) + [random_candidate(), random_candidate()]
best = max(population, key=fitness)

This article explains that loop step by step, on an example you can take in at a glance: a landscape with two hills, where a candidate is a point and its fitness is the height there. First chapter: where evolutionary algorithms sit next to the machine learning and LLMs of part 2. Second: what each step does — with figures that run real, replayable evolutions. Third: the family, evolution in software engineering, and two applications from my own work — the wind farm returns there, as the case study of my master’s thesis.

01Where evolutionary algorithms sit

Evolutionary algorithms are older than deep learning and work quite differently. To place them, ask what a method needs — and what it gives back.

The AI map

“AI” covers methods that have little in common. Some get a formal model of the problem — rules, constraints — and search it: route planners, SAT and SMT solvers. Some get examples and learn a model from them: regression, deep networks, LLMs. And some get only a score: a way to rate any candidate, without knowing why one is better. Evolutionary algorithms belong to the third group; the standard AI textbook files them under search and optimisation, next to hill climbing and simulated annealing [1], not under learning.

The border to machine learning is open, though. Evolution can tune the settings of a model or even grow its network — and if you have used Optuna for hyperparameters, you may already have run an evolutionary algorithm: its CMA-ES and NSGA-II samplers are exactly that.

For the curiousGenetic, local, global: three words that mislead

“Genetic” is a metaphor. Holland’s genetic algorithms [2] borrowed the words — chromosome, gene, crossover, mutation — not the biology. Eiben and Smith warn that reading evolution as optimisation is natural but “somewhat misleading” [3]: nature has no fixed goal. This article says evolutionary algorithm for the whole family and genetic algorithm for the variant with chromosomes and crossover.

Local or global? The textbook calls these methods “local search” because they keep only current candidates, not the paths to them [1]. A population still explores many regions at once — it can climb several hills in parallel.

Learning from data vs searching with a score

A neural network learns by gradient descent: it needs examples with answers and a loss that says, for every weight, in which direction the error shrinks. An evolutionary algorithm needs neither. It needs a score for a candidate — which may itself come from data or from a slow simulation — and it gives back good candidates: a layout, a schedule, a test suite. Nothing is learned that would generalise to the next problem.

Both use randomness on purpose. In part 2, the temperature decides how often a language model picks a less likely word. In an evolutionary algorithm, randomness enters twice: selection gives better candidates better odds, not certainty, and mutation makes random changes no parent had.

The two worlds meet in a new way. In FunSearch [4] and AlphaEvolve [5], building on the idea of evolution through large models [6], the candidates are programs: an LLM reads a few good ones and writes a variant, an automatic evaluator scores it, the best are kept and varied again. The LLM replaces the hand-written mutation — the loop and the score stay exactly as described here.

WORTH IT WHEN

you can score a candidate but have no gradient, the space is huge or oddly shaped, and hundreds of evaluations are affordable.

SOMETHING ELSE IS ENOUGH WHEN

gradients exist, an exact solver fits, or only a few dozen evaluations are affordable. The full list is in the summary.

For the curiousNo Free Lunch

Is there a best optimiser? No: averaged over all possible problems, every black-box optimiser performs the same — Wolpert and Macready’s No Free Lunch theorems [7]. An evolutionary algorithm that knows nothing about your problem has no advantage over random search. The advantage comes from what you build in: an encoding where good parts can be combined, operators that respect the constraints, a score that rewards the right thing. The rest of this article is about exactly these choices.

And one family, the evolution strategies, is closer to gradient descent than it seems: “ES can be seen as computing a finite difference derivative estimate in a randomly chosen direction” [8].

02How an evolutionary algorithm works

The running example: a square landscape, x and y each from 0 to 1, with two hills. A candidate is a point (x, y); its fitness is the height of the landscape there. One hill is broad and low (height 0.6), the other narrow and high (1.0) — the first is easy to find, the second easy to miss. A run starts with random points and evolves them.

The loop

One pass through the loop is a generation; the candidates of a generation are the population; one candidate is a chromosome, its parts are genes — here, the two numbers x and y. Each generation: let the better candidates become parents, make children by mixing and tweaking parents, score the children, and keep the best of old and new. Step through one real generation:

These are the five steps of the general scheme in Eiben and Smith’s textbook [3]. Libraries name and split them differently: some pair the parents up in a step of their own, some first choose which children to mutate, some add random newcomers after the survivors are picked. In my library geneial, each of these steps is an exchangeable building block. The shape stays the same.

Encoding: what a candidate is

The genotype is what the algorithm manipulates; the phenotype is what it means. In the running example the two coincide — two numbers, one point. In most problems they do not, and the encoding is the main design decision: it decides how big the search space is and which mutation and crossover make sense.

The first population is random. When not every genotype is valid — two turbines on the same spot, a city visited twice — either the encoding rules it out, a repair step fixes it, or the score punishes it. The same holds in software engineering, where choosing what a candidate is is most of the work:

Fitness: the score is the specification

The fitness function is the only thing the algorithm knows about your problem. In the running example it is a formula — the height. In real problems it is often a chain of models or a test run, and one evaluation can take minutes or days.

And the algorithm optimises exactly that score, not what you meant. GenProg evolves patches until a failing test passes; it fixed 55 of 105 bugs for about eight dollars each [10]. A later analysis found that most of these patches were not correct — many simply deleted the faulty functionality [12]. The tests were the fitness, and the tests did not forbid deleting. The score is the specification: evolution optimises exactly what you measure.

Three practical points. Budget: the cost of a run is the number of evaluations. The starter at the end of this article evaluates 20 random candidates and then 18 new ones per generation (16 children and 2 newcomers) for 60 generations: 20 + 60 × 18 = 1,100 — at one minute each, about 18 hours. Evaluate in parallel and cache scores. Constraints: invalid candidates can be repaired, penalised in the score, or made impossible by the encoding. Direction: this article maximises; many libraries minimise — negate the score.

For the curiousThe NASA antenna

A good specification can make evolution beat engineers. For NASA’s Space Technology 5 mission, Lohn and colleagues evolved the shape of a small wire antenna. To survive manufacturing tolerances, every design was simulated several times with small random perturbations of its angles and wire radii — and its fitness was the worst of these runs [13]. The result looked like a bent paperclip, met the requirements, and flew on the mission in 2006 — the first evolved hardware in space [14].

Selection: who gets to have children

Selection turns fitness into chances. The classic roulette wheel gives each candidate a slice proportional to its fitness [15]. It has a weakness you can try below: it depends on the absolute numbers. A tournament draws k candidates at random and takes the best — it only compares, and k is a single dial [16]. A softmax with a temperature sits in between, exactly like the token choice of a language model.

Selection pressure

How strongly the better candidates are preferred. Too little, and the search drifts; too much, and the population collapses onto the first good idea.

Elitism copies the best candidates unchanged into the next generation, so the best score can never get worse. But selection should not only pick the best: weaker parents get a chance on purpose, because their differences are what lets the population leave a mediocre hill.

For the curiousTakeover time

How fast does selection alone — without crossover and mutation — fill a population with copies of the best? Goldberg and Deb called this the takeover time and compared the common schemes: proportional selection is slow when the scores are close together, tournament and ranking selection take over in a number of generations that grows only with the logarithm of the population size [16]. Fast takeover is good for refining and bad for exploring.

Crossover: mixing two parents

The selected parents are paired up, and crossover builds a child from both: the hope is that good parts of two parents combine into something better than either. How depends entirely on the encoding. On the two hills, the child takes x from one parent and y from the other — a corner of the rectangle the parents span, as in the loop figure. Numbers in a fixed order can be cut and spliced. The general rule: an operator has to produce valid candidates, or a repair step must fix them. Operators that know the problem usually beat generic ones — the wind-farm case in chapter 03 shows one.

For the curiousBuilding blocks, carefully

Why should crossover work at all? Holland’s schema theorem [2] gives a lower bound for how often short, above-average patterns of genes — schemata — are expected to appear in the next generation. That much is a theorem, under its assumptions. The popular follow-up, the building-block hypothesis — that GAs work by assembling such good blocks into ever better solutions — is contested [3]. One thing is easy to check: standard crossover only reshuffles existing values (on average it leaves their frequency unchanged); new values come from mutation and newcomers.

Mutation: three knobs

Mutation changes a child at random, a little. It is the only step that invents something no parent had — and it can also spoil a good child, which is why usually only some children are mutated. “Mutation rate” sounds like one setting; in fact there are three:

Small steps refine, big steps explore; without mutation, a run freezes wherever crossover has left it. Mutation is also the slot that the LLMs from chapter 01 fill: in FunSearch and AlphaEvolve, the “mutation” is a language model rewriting a program.

For the curiousStep sizes that tune themselves

Choosing the step size by hand is tedious, so evolution strategies let it adapt. Rechenberg’s 1/5 success rule: if more than a fifth of the mutations improve, take bigger steps; if fewer, smaller ones [18]. Self-adaptation puts the step size into the genome, so that it evolves along. CMA-ES goes furthest: it learns a whole covariance matrix — which directions are promising and how they correlate — from the path the search has taken [19].

Survivors, newcomers and diversity

After the children are scored, the survivors are selected. The starter keeps its elite and replaces everybody else with the children; evolution strategies name the alternatives — (μ + λ) picks the survivors from parents and children together, (μ, λ) from the children only [18]. A few random newcomers are added after that choice: they are almost always below average and would not survive a fair comparison. Why bother? Because a population converges when it loses its diversity — when most candidates become alike. If that happens too early, it has converged on the wrong hill.

Exploration vs exploitation

Searching new regions vs refining near what is known to be good. Every knob of the loop moves the balance between the two [3].

Watch both on the two hills — a whole run, generation by generation:

Three ways to read such charts — they are worth logging in any real run. Diversity flat early: too much pressure. The mean no better than random: too many newcomers, the run has become random search. And always compare against random search with the same number of evaluations: here it found the right hill in all 10 runs — but reached its top in only 2 of them, the balanced setting in 6. A good evolutionary algorithm explores and refines.

For the curiousKeeping diversity on purpose

Random newcomers are only one way to keep diversity. Fitness sharing divides the score of candidates that crowd the same spot, so that a crowded hill becomes less attractive; crowding lets a child replace the parent it resembles most, so that niches survive [3]. Novelty search goes all the way and rewards being different instead of being good — and still finds good solutions on deceptive problems [20]. Its descendants, such as MAP-Elites, keep an archive of the best candidate for each kind of behaviour (chapter 03).

When to stop

In most real problems you do not know the best possible score — on the two hills we do, in a wind farm nobody does. So runs stop after a budget: a number of generations, of evaluations, or of seconds; or when the best has not improved for a while; or when a target is reached. Criteria can be combined. The hybrid car in chapter 03 stops after exactly 100 generations, because the controller must answer in fixed time [NWN+15]. Evolutionary algorithms are anytime algorithms: you can stop them whenever you like and take the best so far — and most of the gain usually comes early, as the charts above show.

03Evolutionary algorithms in practice

The family beyond the genetic algorithm, evolution in software engineering, two problems from my own work, and how to start.

The family

“Evolutionary algorithm” is a family name. Its members differ mainly in two choices: what a candidate is — which decides how it can be varied — and what you want back — which decides what survives.

  • Genetic algorithms (GA): strings of genes, crossover as the main operator [15] — the dialect of this article.
  • Evolution strategies (ES) and CMA-ES: real-valued vectors, mutation with adapting step sizes. The default for continuous parameters.
  • Differential evolution (DE): mutates by adding the difference of two other candidates, then crosses with the parent and keeps the better of the two.
  • Genetic programming (GP): candidates are programs — the basis of GenProg and genetic improvement.
  • NSGA-II: several objectives at once; returns a Pareto front of trade-offs instead of one best. In model-driven engineering, PerOpteryx uses it to optimise software architecture models for performance, reliability and cost — see part 1 for models.
  • MAP-Elites and novelty search: keep the best candidate for every kind of behaviour — AlphaEvolve’s program database is inspired by it.
  • NEAT and neuroevolution: evolve neural networks, topology included.
For the curiousEvolution at scale

Evolution strategies also scale like few other methods. Because each candidate is scored independently and only scores need to be exchanged, Salimans and colleagues ran ES on 1,440 workers and taught a simulated humanoid to walk “in under 10 minutes” — a competitive alternative to reinforcement learning with gradients [8]. David Ha’s visual guide shows several of these strategies side by side on 2-D landscapes like our two hills [25].

Evolution in software engineering

In 2001, Harman and Jones proposed to treat software-engineering problems the same way: find a representation for a candidate and a fitness to score it, and let a search do the rest — search-based software engineering [26]. You have met GenProg in chapter 02; three more examples show how far this goes.

  • Test generation. EvoSuite evolves whole test suites for Java classes. A test with x = 5 misses if (x >= 10) by 5; one with x = 9 misses it by only 1. EvoSuite scores that gap, so the search can feel its way toward the branch instead of seeing only “covered / not covered” [9].
  • Faster programs. Genetic improvement evolves edits to existing code: it made a 50,000-line bioinformatics tool 70 times faster on average on its benchmark [27].
  • App testing at scale. Sapienz evolves sequences of user actions and found 558 unknown crashes in the top 1,000 Android apps [28].

Case: wind-farm layout

The problem of the hook, for real: Horns Rev 1 in the Danish North Sea, 80 Vestas V80 turbines on 3.8 × 5 km, wind data of eight years, each layout rated by annual energy production, net present value and internal rate of return. Genetic algorithms are a classic tool for placing wind turbines [29]; my master’s thesis asked a narrower question — how to encode the positions — and compared operators for it [Net17]. Three things make the problem harder than the two hills. The first is the score: behind every turbine the air is slower.

The second is the encoding. Before the real wind model, the thesis tested its operators on toy scores such as “better toward the north-east” on a 1 km site with 12 turbines, at least 240 m apart. For the curiousHow big is the search space, honestly?

The textbook encoding for wind farms is one bit per cell: 1 = a turbine stands here [29]. It is intuitive and fine for coarse grids. But for a fine one the arithmetic explodes: with 10,000 cells per axis, a single chromosome has 108 bits, and a population of 1000 would need hundreds of gigabytes. A list of coordinates stays the same size however fine the grid. And a fun fact on precision: two neighbouring 64-bit floating-point numbers near 1000 m are about 10−13 m apart — far below the size of an atom. No wind model resolves that.

The third is crossover. A layout is a set of positions, not a sequence: which turbine is “gene 3”? The thesis built a problem-aware operator for it:

One-Step crossover does not create a single new position; it only recombines. Problem-aware operators like this one beat the generic ones in the thesis — but only because the simulation reports a score for every turbine. If your evaluator returns one number per candidate, there is nothing to rank by. The general rule: if your evaluator can give partial scores, use them. The thesis concludes that grid genes were much more efficient and gave better results in all its test cases. Read with the caveat in the caption, the lesson is the one from above: match the resolution of the genes to the resolution of the score, and do not pay for precision nobody can see.

Case: energy management in a hybrid car

A plug-in hybrid has two engines on one axle: a combustion engine and an electric motor. Every 0.02 seconds its controller decides how to split the requested torque between them — burn fuel now, or use the battery and recharge it later? In the DFG project OASys at RWTH’s Theory of Hybrid Systems group, we built a controller around a genetic algorithm [NWN+15]; geneial started as its framework.

The weights are the specification, as with GenProg in chapter 02: telling the GA to care only about fuel gave a worse fuel result than adding “keep the battery near its target” — a plausible reading is that the battery term adds foresight beyond the 20-second window. With the best weighting, the GA used up to 1.8 % less fuel than the best of the published reference controllers it was compared with. And smooth driving — the engine should not switch on and off every second — competes with fuel:

For the curiousWhy a GA inside a controller?

Why a GA inside a control loop, where every step has a deadline? Because it is an anytime algorithm with a fixed budget: 50 candidates for 100 generations fit into the time step. Because its population carries over from step to step — shifted by one gene, it is already a good guess for the next decision. And because the fitness can be any mix of goals, including ones that are hard to put into a formula for a classical controller. A larger population starts and ends higher, but costs time; 50 was the compromise for real time.

Try it: settings and libraries

The whole algorithm of the convergence figure, in plain Python without any library — the “balanced” setting on the two hills. Copy it, run it, then change one knob at a time:

import math, random

def fitness(g):  # two hills: broad and low, narrow and high
    x, y = g
    low = 0.6 * math.exp(-((x - 0.25)**2 + (y - 0.30)**2) / 0.08)
    high = 1.0 * math.exp(-((x - 0.80)**2 + (y - 0.75)**2) / 0.006)
    return max(low, high)

def new():  # initialisation: a random candidate
    return [random.random(), random.random()]

def run(seed, size=20, gens=60, elite=2, newcomers=2, k=3, p_mut=0.3, step=0.05):
    random.seed(seed)
    evals = 0
    def score(cands):  # evaluate each candidate once
        nonlocal evals
        evals += len(cands)
        return [(fitness(g), g) for g in cands]
    pop = sorted(score([new() for _ in range(size)]), reverse=True)
    for gen in range(gens):  # stop: a fixed budget of generations
        def pick():  # tournament selection
            return max(random.sample(pop, k))[1]
        kids = []
        for _ in range(size - elite - newcomers):
            a, b = pick(), pick()
            # uniform crossover: each gene from one of the two parents
            child = [random.choice(p) for p in zip(a, b)]
            if random.random() < p_mut:  # mutate some children
                i = random.randrange(2)
                child[i] = min(1, max(0, child[i] + random.gauss(0, step)))
            kids.append(child)
        kids += [new() for _ in range(newcomers)]  # random newcomers
        # elitism + replacement: the best survive, the rest is replaced
        pop = sorted(pop[:elite] + score(kids), reverse=True)
    return pop[0], evals

for seed in range(1, 11):
    (f, best), evals = run(seed)
    print(seed, [round(v, 2) for v in best], round(f, 3), evals)
WHAT YOU WILL SEE

Seeds 4, 5 and 7 end on the low hill with fitness 0.600; seeds 1, 2, 3, 6, 8, 9 and 10 reach the high one (≥ 0.99). Every run costs 1,100 evaluations. Three out of ten on the wrong hill — that is why you run seeds. Try newcomers=0, k=8 or p_mut=0 and count again.

SENSIBLE FIRST SETTINGS

Start from the starter’s defaults — 20 candidates, tournaments of 3, 30 % of the children mutated with a step of 5 % of the range, an elite of 2, 2 newcomers, 60 generations — or from your library’s defaults. Scale the population with the size of the problem. Then run at least ten seeds, log best, mean and diversity, and beat random search with the same budget.

Who tunes the tuner? Every setting is itself a parameter of the search, and choosing them is an optimisation problem of its own — GA configurations are often tuned with GAs. The field distinguishes parameter tuning, fixing the settings before a run, from parameter control, changing them during the run, as self-adapting step sizes do [3]. For real problems, use a library; they differ in what they make easy, and they name the knobs differently:

Many libraries fold the loop into three verbs: ask for new candidates (select, recombine, mutate), evaluate them yourself, tell the algorithm the scores (it decides who survives). Here is that ask–tell loop with CMA-ES for two real numbers (adapted from the cmaes README):

import numpy as np
from cmaes import CMA

def quadratic(x1, x2):
    return (x1 - 3) ** 2 + (10 * (x2 + 2)) ** 2

optimizer = CMA(mean=np.zeros(2), sigma=1.3)
for generation in range(50):
    solutions = []
    for _ in range(optimizer.population_size):
        x = optimizer.ask()                     # propose
        solutions.append((x, quadratic(x[0], x[1])))   # score
    optimizer.tell(solutions)                   # keep the better, adapt

To watch evolution without writing code, try Genetic Cars 2 ↗, where a GA breeds two-wheeled cars and lets you change the mutation rate and the elite while they drive. The library behind my thesis, geneial, is open source (C++11, MIT) [31] but no longer maintained. And if your system already has a digital twin, an optimiser like these can become one of its services.

In short

  • One loop with exchangeable parts: select parents, recombine and mutate, evaluate, select survivors, repeat. You write the encoding and the score; libraries supply the rest.
  • No gradient, no training data — only a score. The price is evaluations: population × generations, each possibly a slow simulation.
  • The encoding is the main design decision. It decides which operators make sense and how big the space is; resolve no finer than the score can tell apart.
  • The score is the specification. Evolution optimises exactly what you measure — patches that delete features, a fuel-only controller that uses more fuel.
  • Selection pressure works against diversity. Every knob moves the balance between exploring and exploiting; a run that loses its diversity has converged, right or wrong.
  • Randomness is a tool, so measure it: run several seeds, log best, mean and diversity, and compare with random search.
WORTH IT WHEN
  • you can score a candidate, but there is no gradient
  • the space is huge, discrete or mixed, with many local optima
  • several objectives pull against each other
  • hundreds to thousands of evaluations are affordable, and “very good” is good enough
SOMETHING ELSE IS ENOUGH WHEN
  • gradients exist, or the problem is convex
  • an exact solver fits the problem — SAT, SMT, integer programming (see SMT-RAT)
  • only a few dozen evaluations are affordable
  • you need a guarantee of optimality
