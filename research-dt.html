02Digital Twins

article · 2026

What a digital twin is — and what it is not: models, digital shadows and services, how a twin acts, and how to build twins with models.

Series · part 1: Model-driven SEpart 2: AI-driven SEpart 3: Digital twinspart 4: Evolutionary algorithms

What a digital twin isInside a twinBuilding twins with models

An injection-moulding machine in a production hall: next to it, a screen shows barrel temperatures, injection pressure and cycle time, live. Is that a digital twin? Many would say yes. By the end of the first chapter you will be able to say why most researchers would not — and what is missing.

“Digital twin” has become one of the most used and least agreed-on words in engineering. This article uses the definition of the RWTH Software Engineering chair and the Internet of Production, worked out by Judith Michael, Bernhard Rumpe, Andreas Wortmann and colleagues, and builds on part 1 of this series: a twin is software, made of models, and it can be engineered with generators. One example carries most of the article: the injection-moulding machine that runs through the chair’s twin papers [16] [DHM+22] — it melts plastic granules and injects them into a mould, here to make door handles; the “rejection rate” is the share of faulty parts. For building twins from engineering data, a wind turbine joins it in chapter 03.

How an injection-moulding machine works

Plastic granules fall from the hopper into a heated barrel. A screw turns inside it: it melts the granules with the heat of the heater bands and its own friction, and it pushes a measured amount of melt to the front (plasticising). Then the screw moves forward like a piston and injects the melt through the nozzle into the closed mould; a holding pressure keeps feeding melt while the part shrinks. The part cools, the clamping unit opens the mould, the part is ejected — and the cycle starts again. The control cabinet and the user interface set temperatures, pressures and times for every phase.

That is why the twin’s data in this article are temperatures of the barrel zones, pressures and the OK / not-OK verdicts on the parts: too hot, and the material degrades; too cold or too little pressure, and the mould is not filled. Getting these settings right by trial and error “takes time, consumes energy, and produces waste” [DHM+22].

01What a digital twin is

First the why, then three words that are often confused — model, shadow, twin — and the definition this article works with.

Systems that outlive their software

Production machines run for ten to fifteen years, wind parks for twenty, bridges for fifty, buildings for up to a hundred. Over that time, they are watched, maintained, adjusted and extended — and the knowledge needed for this is scattered across drawings, sensor logs and people’s heads. “Software systems monitoring civil structures over their lifetime are exposed to the risk of aging much faster than the structures themselves” [20].

A digital twin brings that knowledge together and puts it to work: monitoring what happens, predicting what will happen, simulating what would happen, assisting the people who operate the system and, in the end, controlling it [9]. The precondition is simple and often overlooked: the system needs “observable elements in the physical world that can be monitored, sensed, or actuated and controlled” [2].

Model, shadow, twin: the data-flow view

The most popular way to tell the three words apart comes from Kritzinger and colleagues [14]. It asks a single question: how automatically does data flow between the real system and its digital counterpart? A digital model has no automated data flow — the CAD model of the mould. A digital shadow receives data automatically from the system — the barrel temperatures on a live dashboard. A digital twin also sends data back automatically — a new setpoint for a heater band [10]. (Kritzinger’s own examples are a car’s CAD model and its digital dashboard.) For working purposes: a shadow is a purpose-specific, read-only view on live data; it “can be compared to a view in database systems” [12].

So the live dashboard is a shadow. The ladder is a good first step, but it has two limits. It says nothing about what is inside — “they omit to specify any details on the constituents” [10]. And it gets the case with a person in the loop different from the definition below: when the twin proposes a command and an operator releases it, Kritzinger sees only a shadow, while the chair counts indirect control by informing users [9].

The definition: what a twin consists of

The definition of the RWTH chair and the Internet of Production does not ask about data flow first, but about the parts. It goes back to a survey in the Internet of Production cluster [3] and has been refined several times since; this is the current version 2.2 [1]:

Digital twin · RWTH SE, v2.2 (2024)

A digital twin of an original system is a software that consists of

  • a set of models of the system and
  • a set of digital shadows, both of which are purposefully updated on a regular basis, and
  • provides a set of services to use both purposefully with respect to the original system.

The digital twin interacts with the original system by

  • providing useful information about the system’s context and
  • sending it control commands.

Rumpe, Michael, Wortmann [1]

Read it clause by clause. A twin is a software — not a 3D picture, not a single model. It contains models, because data alone is meaningless: “a structure model helps to understand where sensors are located and thus provides the meta-information for the sensor data” [1]. It contains digital shadows — purpose-bound collections of data (next chapter). Both are purposefully updated on a regular basis: on every event or on a timescale, with a delay that “is very purpose-dependent” [1]. Its services use them — monitoring, prediction, assistance, control. And it interacts with the original system in both directions: it informs, and it commands — “from configurations up to hard real-time execution commands” [1].

The two views fit together once you see that the chair uses the three words differently: in Kritzinger’s ladder, model, shadow and twin are stages; in the chair’s definition, models and shadows are parts of a twin. A shadow can exist on its own, like the live dashboard. A twin always contains shadows. And a twin contains models but is not itself a model — “A DT is not a model itself: instead it comprises models of the system it represents” [6], the way an application contains a class diagram without being one. Its models come from three places: derived from engineering models, the engineering models themselves, or abstracted from the data [1] — the bridge to chapter 03.

Where the definition comes from

The definition has a history. The first version (2020) came out of a survey among the Internet of Production’s 25 departments and 200 researchers [3]: “A digital twin of a system consists of a set of models of the system, a set of digital shadows and their aggregation and abstraction collected from a system, and a set of services that allow using the data and models purposefully with respect to the original system.” A shorter form of 2022 is the one my own papers use [MNN+22] [5]. Version 2.0 added the regular updates and the way back [27]; 2.1 followed discussions in the MDE community at a Dagstuhl seminar [7] [8]; 2.2 (2024) adds “is a software” [1]. For humans, the chair proposes the same definition under another name — a digital companion, because “twin” has other connotations in medicine.

Twin of what, and when?

A twin can exist before its original system is built. During design, the development twin is “a coherent set of development artifacts”; it has “the characteristics of a ‘type’ because it may have a lot of instances” and “does not have data traces, except potentially from simulations” [1]. Once systems are built, each instance gets its own twin — as manufactured, with its tolerances, and as operated, with its wear and tear [FHM+23]. The essay’s example is a car model: the twin of the design, then one twin per car on the road — for usage, for diagnosis, one that monitors like a flight recorder [1]. For our machine type the same holds: one development twin, then one twin for every machine on a shop floor.

Does each purpose get its own twin? The chair answers with a riddle: “Logically, there is only one digital twin. If there were several twins, they would not be twins but triplets or more” [1]. The kinds are kinds of use and service of one logical twin — which may well run distributed over machine, edge and cloud.

In practice, the twin is rarely there from the start: of 356 papers, only 29 develop the twin together with the system, 196 separately [11]. The chair’s position is the opposite — “The digital twin, therefore, is NOT only a spin-off product” [1]. And twins are not only for machines: the chair applies the definition to buildings, hospitals and processes, even to software systems [7].

IS THE WIND-TURBINE COCKPIT A TWIN?

Yes — a development twin: models, a cockpit and agreed parameters of a turbine type, but no traces from a real turbine and no commands yet. More in chapter 03.

Why are there 112 definitions?

A mapping study of 356 papers collected 112 different definitions [26] — and deliberately did not add another: “Instead of producing more of such definitions, we aim to uncover the nature of Digital Twins … bottom-up” [11]. The chair sorts their weaknesses into four kinds: ambiguous (deferring to another undefined term such as “virtual representation”), narrow (one domain or technology), underspecified (only the data flow, as in the ladder) and utopian (“containing all of its physical information”) [5]. Where do they actually disagree?

02Inside a twin

Inside the injection-moulding machine’s twin: what does it keep, how does it decide, and where do people come in?

Digital shadows: data under a purpose

Why define shadows at all? Because a complete digital copy of a factory is out of reach — and not needed. The Internet of Production started from the hypothesis that “a well-designed collection of digital shadows is more promising … than a full-scale digital twin” [13], much like navigation apps “combine abstracted massive live cell phone data with formal map graphs”.

Digital shadow · RWTH SE

A digital shadow includes

  • a set of contextual data traces and/or
  • their aggregation and abstraction collected concerning a system for a specific purpose with respect to the original system.

[2], originally Becker et al. [4]

The key word is purpose: “A DS is designed for fulfilling exactly one specific Purpose” [15]. A shadow is about an asset (the machine), collects data traces from sources — sensors, but also people typing in values — and uses a model to make sense of them [4]. Then it answers. It never acts: “a digital shadow follows the original system under a specific illumination (view), whereas the digital twin follows the original system as well, but also may change the behavior of that system” [FHM+23].

From data to command: the twin’s loop

How does a twin act? In the injection-moulding twin, four components run in a loop [16] [DHM+22]: the data processor collects and prepares data into shadows, the evaluator notices a problem, the reasoner chooses a fix, the executor sends the command — over standard protocols such as MQTT, OPC UA, WebSocket or REST [DHM+22]. What makes it adaptable is that the “intelligence” consists of three kinds of small models: an event says when to act, a goal what should become true, a case how to get there. A new machine needs new cases, not new code.

If no case matches, the reasoner looks for a similar one and synthesises a new case [DHM+22]. Since then, the loop has grown into a richer architecture [8]:

Cockpits and humans in the loop

Digital twin cockpit

A digital twin cockpit is the user interaction part (UI/GUI) of a digital twin. It provides the graphical user interface for visualizations of its data organized in digital shadows and models, and the interaction with services of the digital twin, and thus enabling humans to access, adapt and add information and monitor and partially control the physical system.

    Bano et al. [5], Def. 2

    A first cockpit for the injection-moulding machine showed pressure, temperature and status — and a button for a full machine stop [3]. Who presses the button is a design decision, not a technical one: some users do not want automatic execution, “e.g., risking to stop a complete production line because of wrong parametrization” [9]; in published use cases the operator decides, or picks from a reduced set of options [8]. You saw the switch in the loop above — it changes one step, not the architecture.

    Twins can also guide people instead of machines. Informative services show what is going on; assistive services lead a person step by step — in a smart kitchen, from “a fillable utensil that can hold at least 3 liters” to “left of the stove, in the drawer cabinet, in the first drawer from below” [9]. And a twin can explain itself: instead of “h > 70”, it can say that the vehicle stopped because its battery was overheating [21].

    For the curious

    A process-aware cockpit also knows the steps of a process and who performs each — machine, twin or person. Bano et al. synthesise such cockpits from event logs and tried it on a hospital data set with 25,463 events and 6,455 cases [5]. Note: a cockpit in this sense is not the same as the management cockpit MaCoCo from part 1 — same word, different thing.

    03Building twins with models

    If a twin is software that must live as long as its system, it needs the same discipline as any long-lived software — and models are the way to get it.

    A twin is software, so engineer it

    Two kinds of models meet here: models in twins — of the machine, used while it runs — and models of twins, which describe the twin as a software system [17]. Today, twins are mostly “designed and engineered ad-hoc, in a piecemeal fashion” [FHM+23]. Yet much of what a twin needs already exists: “AutomationML, CAD or STEP files, Modelica models …, or SysML models” [9].

    Generators then do for twins what part 1 showed for information systems: they produce the cockpit, the data structures, and even the connection between the machine and its twin — generated from a few lines that say which sensor port feeds which attribute [6]. Hand-written parts survive regeneration, as with the TOP mechanism. To model a system to begin with, my SysML v2 assistant helps:

    A development twin and its cockpit: wind turbines

    Wind turbines are adapted to every site, and their engineering parameters move between many tools. In the ADD project, they were “exchanged via E-Mail attachments, or as links to a temporary cloud storage” — changes did not propagate fast enough, past projects were hard to reach, and the tools could not read each other’s formats [MNN+22]. Our answer was a single source of truth, generated from models: the turbine’s functional model became a class diagram, and MontiGem with CD2GUI generated the cockpit from it.

    Before an object is released, MontiGem searches for associated objects that are not yet released and lists them, so that a release is always consistent [MNN+22]. The data model of the industry partner had more than 100 classes [MNN+22] and later grew to more than 120 classes and 200 relations [BGK+24]. Is this a digital twin? It is a development twin: it represents the turbine type during design — “as-designed” — and is meant to evolve into twins as manufactured and as operated [MNN+22]. Shadows from a real turbine, services and the way back were not part of the project: “The functional part of the digital twin was out of focus of this project” [MNN+22]. More on the ADD project page.

    Who configures twins in practice?     Twins over decades

    • Composition. Machines form shop floors, factories and networks — and their twins should be composed the same way [FHM+23], which raises questions of integration, intellectual property, rights and roles [18].
    • Evolution. A twin that lives for decades needs a way back from operation into the next development cycle [8] [17] — and its software will be rebuilt several times on the way, which is where models and generators pay off.
    • AI in and around twins. Process mining and AI are services of a twin [2]; and LLMs can write the models twins are built from — in my dissertation, GPT-4 produced a valid class diagram for a “Digital Twin of a Hydraulic Press” in 19 of 20 runs [Net24]. See part 2.

    In short

    • A twin is software made of models and digital shadows plus services, and it interacts with its original system — it is “not only a simulation model, it is not only a dashboard, and it is not just another term for a CPS” [9].
    • A shadow is passive and serves one purpose, like a view in a database; the twin also acts.
    • Two views of three words: the ladder asks how automatically data flows; the chair’s definition asks what is inside — models and shadows are parts of a twin, not stages before it.
    • Humans stay in the loop: the cockpit is the twin’s interface, and whether a command runs automatically is a design decision.
    • Engineer twins model-driven: a twin must live as long as its system, so build it from models and generators — cockpits, connections, whole platforms; a development twin can even start before the product exists.
    • “Digital twin” is overloaded: 112 definitions; ask which one someone means.
    A TWIN IS WORTH IT WHEN
    • there are elements you can observe, measure or control [2]
    • the system lives long enough to repay the effort [9]
    • someone will act on what it says — automatically or through people
    A SHADOW MAY BE ENOUGH WHEN
    • you only need to watch or analyse [13]
    • users do not want automatic control [9]
    • there is no way to act back on the system
