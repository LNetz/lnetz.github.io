    What a digital twin is — and what it is not: models, digital shadows and services, how a twin acts, and how to build twins with models.

    Series1Model-driven engineering2AI in software engineering3Digital twins4Evolutionary algorithms

    01What a digital twin is02How a twin works03Building twins with modelsIn shortLiterature

    Your electric car’s app shows its charge level and range, live, wherever you are. Is that a digital twin? Many would say yes. By the end of the first chapter you will be able to say why that alone is insufficient under the definition used here — and what is missing.

    “Digital twin” has become one of the most used and least agreed-on words in engineering. This article uses the definition of the Software Engineering group at RWTH Aachen and the Internet of Production, a large research cluster at RWTH Aachen on digital production [1]. It builds on part 1 of this series: a twin is software, made of models, and it can be engineered with generators. Chapter 01 explains the concepts on a car. Chapter 02 shows how a twin works on the case study of the RWTH group’s papers, the injection-moulding machine in the figure above. Chapter 03 builds twins with models, with wind turbines as a second case study.

    01What a digital twin is

    The example for this chapter is something you know: a car. First the why, then three words that are often confused — model, shadow, twin — the definition, and the difference between the twin of a car model and the twin of your car.

    Systems that outlive their software

    Production machines run for ten to fifteen years, bridges for fifty, buildings for up to a hundred. Over that time they are watched, maintained, adjusted and extended — and the knowledge needed for this is scattered across drawings, sensor logs and people’s heads. The software that watches over such a structure ages much faster than the structure itself [2].

    A digital twin brings that knowledge together and puts it to work: monitoring what happens, predicting what will happen, simulating what would happen, assisting the people who operate the system and, in the end, controlling it. The precondition is an identifiable original system whose relevant states or events can be observed and, where appropriate, influenced. Physical assets are common examples, but the definition also permits workflows and software systems [5].

    Model, shadow, twin: the data-flow view

    The most popular way to tell the three words apart comes from Kritzinger and colleagues [6]. It asks a single question: how automatically does data flow between the real system and its digital counterpart? A digital model has no automated data flow — the CAD model of your car. A digital shadow receives data automatically — the charge level in your app. A digital twin also sends data back automatically — say, a new charging limit, computed from the battery data and sent to the car without anyone typing it in.

    So the app is a shadow. The ladder is a good first step, but it has two limits. It says nothing about what a twin consists of [8]. And it disagrees with the definition used here about one case: if a person carries out what the twin proposes, Kritzinger calls it a shadow. Under RWTH, human-mediated control can count, provided models, regularly updated shadows and appropriate services are also present. Human involvement alone does not establish a twin.

    The definition: what a twin consists of

    The RWTH definition does not ask about data flow first, but about the parts. This article uses version 2.2 (2024):

    Digital twin · RWTH SE, v2.2 (2024)

    A digital twin of an original system is a software that consists of

    • a set of models of the system and
    • a set of digital shadows, both of which are purposefully updated on a regular basis, and
    • provides a set of services to use both purposefully with respect to the original system.

    The digital twin interacts with the original system by

    • providing useful information about the system’s context and
    • sending it control commands.

    Rumpe, Michael, Wortmann [1]

    This component-and-interaction definition describes an operational twin. The same research calls coherent engineering artifacts created before the original exists a development twin, a separate lifecycle notion that need not yet have live shadows or commands. Read the operational clauses one by one. A twin is a software — not a 3D picture, not a single model. It contains models, because data alone means little: a structure model tells where each sensor sits, and so what its readings are about. It contains digital shadows — purpose-bound collections of data (below). Both are purposefully updated on a regular basis: at cadences appropriate to their purpose. Shadows may follow sensor events in seconds; engineering models may change after a repair or redesign. Identity, timestamps and synchronization policies connect these different cadences. Its services use them — monitoring, prediction, assistance, control. And it interacts with the original system in both directions: it informs, and it commands — from configurations up to hard real-time commands.

    How do the ladder and the definition fit together? They use the same three words differently:

    THE LADDER

    model → shadow → twin are stages, ordered by how automatically data flows.

    THE RWTH DEFINITION

    models and shadows are parts of a twin.

    A shadow can exist on its own, like the app. An operational twin under this definition contains shadows. And a twin contains models but is not itself a model [10] — the way an application contains a class diagram without being one. Its models come from engineering models or are abstracted from the data, which is where chapter 03 picks up.

    For the curiousWhere the definition comes from

    The definition has a history. The first version (2020) came out of a survey among the Internet of Production’s 25 departments and 200 researchers [11]: “A digital twin of a system consists of a set of models of the system, a set of digital shadows and their aggregation and abstraction collected from a system, and a set of services that allow using the data and models purposefully with respect to the original system.” A shorter form of 2022 is the one my own papers use. Version 2.0 added the regular updates and the way back; 2.1 followed discussions in the model-driven engineering community at a Dagstuhl seminar; 2.2 (2024) adds “is a software”. For people, the RWTH group proposes the same definition under another name — a digital companion, because “twin” means something else in medicine [1].

    Digital shadows: data under a purpose

    Why define shadows at all? Because a complete digital copy of a car — let alone a factory — is out of reach, and not needed. The Internet of Production started from the hypothesis that a well-designed collection of digital shadows is more promising than a full-scale digital twin [12] — much like a navigation app, which combines abstracted live data from many phones with a map.

    Digital shadow · RWTH SE

    A digital shadow includes

    • a set of contextual data traces and/or
    • their aggregation and abstraction collected concerning a system for a specific purpose with respect to the original system.

    SE@RWTH [5]

    The key word is purpose: shadows are created or selected for a specific purpose, which may overlap with others. A SQL view illustrates a purpose-specific projection, not the entire contextual shadow: identity, source, units, timestamps, latency and provenance also matter. It is about an asset (your car), collects data traces from sources — sensors, but also people typing in values — and uses a model to make sense of them. Then it answers a question. It never acts: a shadow follows the original system under one particular view; a twin follows it too, but may also change its behaviour [FHM+23].

    One type, many instances

    A twin can exist before its original system is built. While a car model is being designed, its development twin is a coherent set of development artefacts. It has the character of a type, because it may have many instances, and it has no data from a real car, except perhaps from simulations [1]. Once cars are built, each one can have its own instance twin — as manufactured, with its tolerances, and as driven, with its wear. If you write software: the type/instance analogy is useful, but lifecycle twins are not literally classes and objects.

    Does each purpose get its own twin? The RWTH group answers with a riddle: “Logically, there is only one digital twin. If there were several twins, they would not be twins but triplets or more” [1]. In software terms: one logical system, possibly deployed as several services — in the car, at the edge, in the cloud.

    For the curiousWhy there are 112 definitions

    A mapping study of 356 papers collected 112 different definitions [15]. Their weak spots fall into four kinds: ambiguous (they defer to another undefined term such as “virtual representation”), narrow (one domain or technology), underspecified (only the data flow, as in the ladder) and utopian (a complete copy of everything). Where they part ways, this article takes one side:

    QuestionThe stance taken here
    Does data flow back?Yes — directly or through people. Some definitions only mirror the system.
    A complete copy?No — a twin is always an abstraction for purposes. Early definitions aimed at describing a product completely.
    Passive or active?Active: a twin is a running software system, not a data structure synchronised from outside.
    Before the product exists?Yes: the development twin. In practice, though, 266 of the 356 papers address the operation phase, and only 29 develop the twin together with the system [16].

    02How a twin works

    The case study of this chapter is the injection-moulding machine that runs through the RWTH group’s twin papers [17]: what does its twin keep, how does it decide, and where do people come in?

    Meet the machine

    An injection-moulding machine makes plastic parts — here, door handles. Plastic granules fall from the hopper into a heated barrel. A screw turns inside it: it melts the granules with the heat of the heater bands and its own friction, and pushes a measured amount of melt to the front. Then the screw moves forward like a piston and injects the melt into the closed mould. The part cools, the clamping unit opens the mould, the part is ejected — and the cycle starts again. The control cabinet and the user interface set temperatures, pressures and times for every phase.

    So the twin’s data are temperatures of the barrel zones, pressures and the verdict OK / not OK for each part; the rejection rate is the share of faulty parts. Too hot, and the material degrades; too cold or too little pressure, and the mould is not filled. Finding the right settings by trial and error takes time, consumes energy and produces waste [DHM+22].

    From data to command: the twin’s loop

    A thermostat is the smallest loop: measure, compare with the setpoint, switch the heater. A twin’s loop is the same, but each step is a replaceable model. In the injection-moulding twin, four components run in a loop: the data processor collects data and prepares it into shadows, the evaluator notices a problem, the reasoner chooses a fix, the executor sends the command — over standard protocols such as MQTT, OPC UA or REST. The intelligence consists of three kinds of small models: an event says when to act, a goal what should become true, a case how to get there. For a supported machine interface, new cases can adapt behavior without changing the core loop; new protocols, sensors or actuators may still require code. [DHM+22].

    If no case matches, the reasoner looks for a similar one and adapts it into a new case. The shadow in step 2 is a view like the ones on the car in chapter 01: the last two readings of one sensor, for one purpose.

    For the curiousFrom one loop to many services

    Since then, the loop has grown into a richer architecture [18]:

    People in the loop: cockpits

    Twins can guide people, not only machines. Informative services show what is going on; assistive services lead a person step by step — in a smart kitchen, from “a fillable utensil that can hold at least 3 liters” to “left of the stove, in the drawer cabinet, in the first drawer from below” [4].

    And a twin can explain itself: instead of “h > 70”, it can say that the vehicle stopped because its battery was overheating [19]. All of this needs a user interface — the twin’s cockpit:

    Digital twin cockpit

    A digital twin cockpit is the user interaction part (UI/GUI) of a digital twin. It provides the graphical user interface for visualizations of its data organized in digital shadows and models, and the interaction with services of the digital twin, and thus enabling humans to access, adapt and add information and monitor and partially control the physical system.

    Bano et al. [20], Def. 2

    A first cockpit for the injection-moulding machine showed pressure, temperature and status — and a button for a full machine stop [11]. Who may issue a command is an operational and technical design decision: authorization, interlocks, safe limits and audit logs must support it. in published use cases the operator decides, or picks from a reduced set of options. You saw the switch in the loop above — it changes one step, not the architecture.

    For the curiousProcess-aware cockpits

    A process-aware cockpit also knows the steps of a process and who performs each — machine, twin or person. Such cockpits can be synthesised from event logs; the approach was tried on a hospital data set with 25,463 events and 6,455 cases [20]. Note: a cockpit in this sense is not the same as the management cockpit MaCoCo from part 1 — same word, different thing.

    03Building twins with models

    If a twin must preserve continuity across several software generations, it needs the same discipline as any long-lived software — and models are the way to get it.

    A twin is software, so engineer it

    Twins use models in two ways: models inside the twin describe the machine; models of the twin describe the twin’s own software — and those are the ones we generate code from. Today, twins are mostly designed and built ad hoc, piece by piece [FHM+23]. Yet much of what a twin needs already exists: AutomationML, CAD or STEP files, Modelica or SysML models.

    Generators then do for twins what part 1 showed for information systems: they can produce cockpit components, data schemas and adapter skeletons when transformation rules and integration contracts are specified — generated from a few lines that say which sensor port feeds which attribute [10]. Hand-written parts survive regeneration, as with the TOP mechanism.

    Language models can draft such models, too: in my dissertation, GPT-4 produced a valid class diagram for a “Digital Twin of a Hydraulic Press” in 19 of 20 runs [Net24]. More in part 2.

    A platform that builds twins

    WordPress builds websites; a site owner then builds pages without writing code. The same split works for twins. Who configures twins in practice? Mostly domain experts without a software-engineering education — our 2022 study addressed a gap in low-code configuration of twin behavior for these users [DHM+22]. So we generated a low-code platform for twins: MontiGem builds the workshop, and the twin designer uses the workshop to build twins. Three roles share the work:

    Our 2022 comparison covered related technologies with different roles: Eclipse Ditto is an open-source twin framework, AWS IoT Greengrass an edge runtime ecosystem, Microsoft DTDL a modeling language, and Siemens MindSphere an industrial IoT offering. Our contribution focused on domain experts configuring behavior in a generated low-code environment. The comparison describes the assessed workflows at that time, not a current market-wide limitation. Our approach is one possible implementation, and one question stays open: one big twin, or many separate ones?

    Case study: a development twin for wind turbines

    Wind turbines are adapted to every site, and their engineering parameters move between many tools. In the ADD project they were exchanged as e-mail attachments or links to temporary cloud storage — changes did not propagate fast enough, and the tools could not read each other’s formats [MNN+22]. Our answer was a single source of truth, generated from models: the turbine’s functional model became a class diagram, and MontiGem with CD2GUI generated the cockpit from it.

    Is this a digital twin? The paper presents a development-oriented twin prototype: an engineering information backbone for the turbine type, like the design example in chapter 01. It demonstrated model-derived CRUD views and engineering-tool writeback, not operational telemetry shadows, adaptive runtime services or turbine control under the operational v2.2 criteria. Details are on the ADD project page.

    Twins over decades

    • Composition. A car is made of a powertrain, the powertrain of a battery, and many cars form a fleet — twins should be composed the same way, which raises questions of integration, intellectual property, rights and roles [21].
    • Evolution. A twin that lives for decades needs a way back from operation into the next development cycle [22] — and its software will be rebuilt several times on the way, which is where models and generators pay off.
    • AI in and around twins. Process mining and AI are services of a twin; and language models can write the models twins are built from (part 2).

    Part-of composition alone does not make twins interoperable: identity, units, time bases, ownership and service contracts must align. Several views can share one logical twin; independently governed twins need explicit integration. A digital thread emphasizes lifecycle traceability, while a cyber-physical system combines computation with a physical process; neither term alone establishes the twin services described here.

    In short

    • A twin is software made of models and digital shadows plus services, and it interacts with its original system — it is “not only a simulation model, it is not only a dashboard, and it is not just another term for a CPS” [4].
    • A shadow is passive and is selected for a purpose, with SQL projections as one implementation analogy; the twin also acts.
    • Two views of three words: the ladder asks how automatically data flows; the RWTH definition asks what is inside — models and shadows are parts of a twin, not stages before it.
    • Type and instance: development and operational twins describe different lifecycle perspectives; class/object is only an analogy.
    • Humans stay in the loop: the cockpit is the twin’s interface, and whether a command runs automatically is a design decision.
    • Engineer twins model-driven: continuity across software generations is a design goal. Models and generators help build components; integration and runtime validation remain engineering work. Lifecycle data may be archived after asset retirement.
    A TWIN IS WORTH IT WHEN
    • there is an identifiable original system with relevant observable states or events
    • the system lives long enough to repay the effort
    • someone will act on what it says — automatically or through people
    A SHADOW MAY BE ENOUGH WHEN
    • you only need to watch or analyse
    • no twin service needs to initiate an intervention, even through a person
    • there is no way to act back on the system
